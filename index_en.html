Research Profile

My research centers on state-owned capital mergers and acquisitions (M&A), examining how government actors allocate capital, shape corporate innovation, and affect strategic emerging industries. I combine empirical causal inference methods (PSM-DID, event studies) with multi-case qualitative analysis to build analytical frameworks for M&A governance in transition economies. My work spans three interconnected streams: (1) the boundary of government action in state capital allocation, (2) the role of patient capital in long-term innovation, and (3) the economic consequences of extreme climate events.

Publications

[1] Still Waters Run Deep: Long-Term State Capital Participation and Innovation in Private Firms in Strategic Emerging Industries Accepted
Shanmin Li, Ying Kuang, Fenghui Lai
Management Review (管理评论), accepted July 9, 2026  |  CSSCI / PKU Core / NSFC Class-A Journal
Abstract: Can state capital participation in private firms promote long-term innovation? The key is the holding horizon. Using Chinese A-share private firms in strategic emerging industries (2008–2022) and a PSM-DID design, we identify the innovation effects of long-term state capital participation. Two mechanisms emerge: (1) a debt-maturity matching channel — state capital improves credit expectations and extends debt maturity, providing stable financing for long-horizon R&D; (2) a managerial myopia mitigation channel — long-term equity investors alleviate short-termism, encouraging investment in foundational research and high-quality patents. Further analysis shows government guidance funds amplify these effects, while local GDP growth pressures attenuate them. By distinguishing the heterogeneous effects of state capital participation through the lens of holding duration, this study extends mixed-ownership reform theory and offers direct policy implications for building patient capital institutions.
[2] National Strategic Orientation and M&A Activity by State-Owned Enterprises Under Review
Shanmin Li, Ying Kuang (Corresponding Author), Zhihong Huang
Submitted to International Journal of the Economics of Business
Abstract: How does national strategic orientation drive M&A decisions by state-owned enterprises (SOEs)? Matching China's Five-Year Plan industry classifications with SOE-listed firm M&A data, we construct a strategic orientation index and examine its effects on deal volume, target characteristics, and performance. Results show that strategic orientation significantly increases SOE M&A activity, especially in strategic emerging industries and critical supply chain segments. This "national strategy-to-M&A behavior" transmission mechanism reveals that SOE acquisitions operate not merely as market transactions but as instruments of national industrial policy.

Working Papers

[3] The Government Action Boundary in State-Owned Capital M&A: A Contingency Governance Framework Based on Asset Strategic Attributes and Institutional Friction Manuscript in preparation
Shanmin Li, Ying Kuang, Yilin Zhang (Lingnan College, Sun Yat-sen University; Corresponding Author)
Abstract: Government intervention in state-owned capital M&A is not the opposite of markets: the appropriate boundary depends on the interaction between asset strategic importance and institutional friction. Drawing on eight cases — including Baowu Steel (central SOE strategic restructuring), Liaoning Port Group (cross-jurisdictional integration), China Merchants Property (central SOE specialized consolidation), and Guangxin Group (reverse mixed-ownership reform) — we construct a two-stage "property rights allocation–operational logic" framework. Core finding: government should lead property rights reconfiguration, while operations should be returned to market mechanisms. When asset strategic importance is extremely high and market mechanisms are unavailable (no pricing mechanism, no pluralistic buyers/sellers, no exit channels), government substitution is both rational and efficient. When market mechanisms are available but blocked by administrative barriers, government should act as a "barrier-clearing coordinator" and then exit. We identify four governance modes with contingency conditions and match intervention tools to different types of "institutional losers."
[4] Extreme Climate Events, Foreign Direct Investment, and Supply Chain Resilience Data collection
Ying Kuang
Abstract: How do extreme climate events affect Chinese supply chain resilience through FDI channels? This study constructs a matched dataset of extreme climate disasters and foreign-invested enterprise supply chain networks. It identifies the micro-level mechanisms through which climate shocks propagate via cross-border capital flows and global value chains to local supply chains, providing new analytical tools for industrial security assessment under climate risk.
